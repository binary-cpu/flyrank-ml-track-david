Which pages should you refresh first? Ranking next-month search decline from one month of Search Console data

Capstone research paper · FlyRank ML Internship track · Lane: Content Refresh Prioritization

IntroductionDataMethodologyResultsLimitationsRecommendationsReproducibilityCredit
Abstract. Content teams cannot refresh every page, so which ones should they look at first? I used one month (March 2026) of daily Search Console metrics for 101,441 content items across 44 client sites to rank pages by the chance their impressions fall by 20% or more the next month. I compared a transparent rule, a one-feature rule, Logistic Regression and Random Forest on a split where each client appears only in train or only in test. Random Forest reached AUC 0.673, against 0.648 for Logistic Regression and 0.612 for the best simple rule. The ranking is a decision-support aid for human reviewers, not a forecast and not evidence that a refresh will recover traffic.

Introduction

An editor with time for 500 pages a week needs an order to review them in. Picture a site with thousands of pages. In this data, 0.517 of pages lost at least 20% of their impressions in a month, so they cannot all be treated as urgent, and reading every one is not possible.

This paper asks whether signals visible in one month of search data can put the pages that go on to lose visibility nearer the top of that list, and whether a model does so better than a simple rule.

Data

Source. The FlyRank ML Internship warehouse release (access-gated). Tables used. fact_content_daily_performance (daily search impressions, clicks and average position per content item and client; 20,266,108 daily rows across the two months used) and dim_content (content update date).

Windows. Features come from March 2026 only; the outcome is April 2026. The final month of the release was kept sealed and never used.

Kept. Content items with at least 100 March impressions and a measured position: 101,441 items, 44 clients. Excluded and why. Product-computed flags and scores (they would be circular); identifiers as model inputs (used only to group clients); Google Analytics fields, available for only 4.2% of March rows.

Methodology

Label. An item “declined” if its April impressions were below 80% of its March impressions (base rate 0.517). This is an observed proxy for decline, not a cause.

Features (March only). Impressions, click-through rate, average position, days with impressions, days since last content update, and a flag for unknown update date. Update dates falling after March were treated as unknown, because they cannot have been known in March.

Baselines. The week-4 rule scores pages that are at least 180 days stale and have 500+ impressions. A second rule ranks lowest click-through rate first; it is the bar any model should clear.

Validation. The primary test set holds out whole clients (11 clients, 8,356 items; test base rate 0.484). Five client-grouped folds check stability. Metrics: AUC, and precision among the top 50 and top 500 ranked items, shown next to the base rate.

Leakage checks. No April-derived column is a feature; no product flags; identifiers excluded. Random Forest AUC was 0.675 under a random split and 0.673 under the client-grouped split. As a harness check, deliberately adding April impressions raised AUC to 0.917, so a real leak would have been visible.

Limitations and honest framing

One month-pair. Results come from March to April 2026 and 44 clients. Seasonality, algorithm changes or demand shifts could cause declines the features cannot see.

A proxy label. A 20% impression drop is a convention, not a verdict on content quality. No causal claim is made: nothing here shows a refresh would reverse a decline.

Weak staleness data. 82.2% of items had no update date verifiable as of March, so staleness-based reasons rarely fire. Among known dates, 96.2% share the same value (4 days), which looks like a pipeline artifact rather than real editing history.

Possible measurement artifacts. 37.2% of items show exactly zero clicks despite 100+ impressions, and 60% of the top 500 ranked items are among them, so the highest-ranked pages may reflect missing click data rather than real behavior. 0.3% of items also have an average position below 1, which cannot occur in raw Search Console data, so position is best read as a relative signal. Check measurement before acting.

Limited signal. Random Forest AUC was 0.673 and precision@500 was 0.61 against a 0.484 base rate, so many top-ranked pages still did not decline. Use it as a ranking aid with human review, a directional starting point only.

Cost and value

Reviewing the top 500 items surfaced pages that declined 0.62 of the time versus a 0.517 base rate: about 49 more declining pages found than random review, and about 192 of 500 reviews on pages that did not decline. This measures ranking value, not traffic recovered.

Human review required

Before acting, check whether the page is deliberately frozen or evergreen, was recently optimized, or has a business reason (season, redesign, compliance) for the pattern, or may have missing click tracking.

Do not automate

No auto-publishing, unpublishing or deindexing from this score; no sole use for resourcing decisions; no use on other clients or lanes without re-validation; the score is a ranking signal, not a certified probability.

Monitoring

Re-validate monthly on the newest month-pair. Retrain if AUC falls below 0.60, if the monthly decline base rate moves by more than 10 points, or if unknown update dates keep growing.
